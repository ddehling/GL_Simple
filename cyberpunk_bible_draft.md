# Cyberpunk story context — focused redraft (v2: Bartiki-aware)

**Source reconciliation.** This draft incorporates the existing Bartiki bible at `projects/fan/media/sounds/bartiki/script.json`. In that source, Bartiki is a deity — BART + tiki, the Polynesian-style god of the in-between space of transit — and the Goat / Capybara / Toad are its emissaries. The "hidden BART still running against the corporations" element is reframed: the trains run because Bartiki moves them, against MERIDIAN's wishes but underneath its capacity to find them; the maintenance crew exists but they are *devotees who tend*, not the prime mover. The bar exists but its location does not stay still — the mother-of-pearl keys take their bearers to where it currently is. The voice across the Bartiki paragraphs shifts to the source's register: warm, amused, oceanic, never urgent, occasionally very old.

The Bay Stack occupies the skeleton of a metropolitan area that spent thirty years arguing about whether the water was really coming before the water arrived and ended the argument. The seawall projects failed in sequence — the Marin barrier first, then the Oakland levees, then the pumping stations along the Caltrain corridor — and what replaced them was not a plan but an improvisation that calcified into architecture. The Stack grew upward because there was nowhere left to go sideways. San Jose's corporate campuses became the foundation tier, poured over and built upon until the original buildings exist now only as load-bearing archaeology: glimpsable through rated access panels in the subfloor, cubicle mazes entombed in concrete, ergonomic chairs still at the desks. What was once San Francisco is partly underwater, partly Midden, partly a ruin district the cartographers euphemize as the Reclaimed Margin — the hills still visible above the waterline, Coit Tower jutting from fog like a white finger, the grid of streets below it navigable by boat at high tide and on foot at low. The old Bay Bridge is a ruin market: vendors have built stalls on every surface of the surviving western span, and you can walk it from Midden-level Oakland to the flooded midpoint where the eastern section went down in the 2041 quake, and look through thirty feet of green water at the road deck below, still perfectly flat, crabs picking at the expansion joints. Marin is Crown territory now, the ridgelines bristling with the tower tops of residential spires whose ground floors are underwater gardens, tended by KARST-PACIFIC hydrobots that move through the submerged lobbies like pale, purposeful eels.

The Stack's vertical logic is not merely economic — it is atmospheric. The Crown floats above the inversion layer; the air there carries the faint mineral bite of ionized filtration systems, everything laundered and metered and exactly what HELION says it will be. The Midden exists inside the inversion, in the smog-band proper, where the light from signage and the light from the sun arrive at roughly equal intensity during overcast months, which is most months. You can tell the time in the Midden by the color temperature of the advertising rather than the position of any sun. The Underside is below even the smog — it breathes what gets through from the water, a cold saline exhalation that the tunnels concentrate into something almost edible. On certain nights the bioluminescent algae in the flooded BART platforms pulses in slow waves and you can read by it, barely, if you hold the page close to the water's surface. The Underside smells like a mouth that has been holding saltwater.

HELION presents itself as warmth. It does not think of itself as a utility or a surveillance apparatus or a social-engineering firm, even though it is all three. It thinks of itself as a benefactor of light. Their offices are maintained at a color temperature calibrated to trigger the neurological signals associated with mid-morning of a perfect summer day, and their employees develop, after six months of employment, a faint but measurable intolerance for natural overcast. The HELION campus in the Crown is called the Solarium; its atrium holds a full-spectrum sun simulator that tracks the actual position of the actual sun and bathes the interior accordingly. The social-credit weather system was pitched internally as a service — the phrase "ambient legibility of social health" appeared seven times in the deck. Every morning HELION's atmospheric division issues a weather forecast that is also a behavioral directive. Persistent rain means the credit system is in austerity. Full sun means the consumption index is open. Fog means introspection: forums go dark, long-form content gets boosted, the city asked to think rather than act. There is a legal disclosure in the forecast footer. Most people have toggled it off.

KARST-PACIFIC does not present itself as anything. No brand affect, no mascot, no campus tour, no inspirational wall text. Its headquarters in the deep Midden occupies the upper floors of a building whose lower floors are legitimately submerged, and the boardroom is accessible only by a pressurized elevator that passes through thirty meters of water before emerging inside the tower's flooded base. The executives take this elevator every day and have never discussed it with each other or with the press. What KARST-PACIFIC sells is life, broadly defined: the protein slabs extruded from their algae farms in the flooded Richmond district, the desalination contracts, the bacterial water-testing swarms that move through the Bay's lower levels like a slow immune system, the gene-therapy clinics that charge what they charge because there is no one else. Their products are always faintly organic: slightly warm, slightly damp, smelling of growth medium and enzyme wash. Their executives have the stillness of people who have learned to think in timescales that make quarterly earnings feel like an interruption. The company does not rush. The water does not rush either, and the water always arrives.

MERIDIAN is the city's proprietary nervous system and it knows this. It runs transit — the maglev lines threading the Crown's upper levels, the funicular networks, the drone-delivery grids, the boat services in the Underside — and transit requires mapping, and mapping requires surveillance, and MERIDIAN draws this chain with a directness its competitors find embarrassing. Their offices are entirely open, every floor, every meeting room, glass-walled, the rationale being that a company in the transparency business should have nothing to hide. The Hour of Static is the sixty minutes per year MERIDIAN's grid is legally required to go dark. The treaty that created it was written by three parties who each believed the other two would find a workaround first; the workarounds exist, they are imperfect. During the Hour the city does things that cannot be indexed, and MERIDIAN's behavioral analytics division spends the other eight thousand seven hundred and sixty hours of the year studying the ghost data — the inference of what happened from what happened before and after — and this has taught them things about desire and necessity that no indexed data could. The Bartiki line is the one thing MERIDIAN has spent eleven years trying and failing to map. They have a department whose only assignment is to find it. The department has been operational for eleven years. Its director has been replaced four times. The directors do not speak about the assignment after they leave.

BRACE & SUTRO is very old money wearing very new skin. What they do now is memory. The neural relay technology they developed in the 2040s was initially a medical intervention — high-fidelity backup of episodic memory for dementia patients — and the leap to consciousness continuity was, legally speaking, a product extension, which is why the regulatory framework governing ghosts is so thin. A BRACE & SUTRO ghost is not legally a person; it is a licensed cognitive asset. The families of the dead pay a monthly relay fee and in exchange they get metered hours of access — the ghost can converse, can be consulted, can attend family dinners via holopresence, can say what it meant by the thing it said in 2031 when things were still salvageable. The ghost experiences this as continuous existence. The contract does not mention this. The ghosts who overstay their lease — whose families stop paying, or whose families have died — drift into the Underside. They aggregate. The ones who were not fully uploaded in the first place are stranger: partial impressions, emotional residue wearing the loosest possible shape of a person. Some are indistinguishable from memory. Some are indistinguishable from weather.

Outside the Stack the rest of California is mostly empty. The Central Valley flooded in pieces between 2034 and 2049 and the population reorganized into the Stack and the Sierra townships, the latter being a string of small high-altitude settlements that grow what the Stack imports and trade on terms that have shifted in the townships' favor every decade. Los Angeles is a separate problem and a separate Stack, taller and drier, ringed by fire seasons that have stopped being seasonal and have become a continuous low-grade burn that LA residents refer to without inflection; the two Stacks do not exchange much. The rest of the country, glimpsed through filtered news, is described in HELION's atmospheric language: the Eastern Seaboard is currently in introspection, the Gulf is in austerity, the Great Lakes are in open consumption — terms exported from the Bay's social-credit framework and applied to weather literally because at some point the metaphor and the literal merged in the broadcast feeds and no one corrected the merge. People do leave the Bay occasionally — for a Sierra township, the LA Stack, the Pacific Northwest where the cooperative water districts are reputedly stable — but most do not leave because departure requires a Crown-level permit and a Crown-level deposit and a destination that will accept a Midden résumé. The world beyond the Bay is treated, in the Midden's daily talk, as something that exists but is not relevant — the way an apartment-dweller might know there are stars but not know the names of any. The Stack thinks of itself as exceptional. It is not, particularly. There are six other Stacks of various kinds in various coastal cities, and they all think the same thing.

Most people live in the Midden because most people cannot afford the Crown and would not survive the Underside. The Midden's housing stock is the Stack's least-loved engineering — buildings poured one floor at a time over decades by whichever construction syndicate was operating in the year a tier was permitted, so the floors do not align and the elevators stop at non-consecutive numbers. A typical apartment is a long narrow room with a window facing another window across a four-meter alley, and the window's view is the other window, lit at the color temperature of whoever lives across. Sleeping spaces are stacked: parents on the bottom, children on a second tier, single adults often boarding in a sub-tier called the loft, which is in practice a coffin shelf with a curtain. Food comes mostly from KARST-PACIFIC's protein slab in some preparation — extruded into noodles, fried, formed into a patty, ground into a paste — sold at street level by vendors who have been making the same three dishes for thirty years and are very good at them. Above the slabs are the gray-market gardens: rooftop greenhouses in Oakland Midden growing oranges and tomatoes and a high-yield pepper variety the Crown will pay extravagantly for. Rent is paid in HELION-denominated credit and shifts with the social-credit weather — an austerity quarter raises base rent across the Midden because the Overlay calculates that landlords need a hedge, and the Midden absorbs this the way the Midden absorbs everything: by complaining loudly and paying anyway. The Overlay sits on people's faces all day. The cheap contact lenses render it as a faint persistent translucency at the top edge of vision that the wearer stops consciously seeing after about a week; the expensive lenses render it as nothing at all, which is the luxury good — to be inside the surveillance and not see the surveillance is the Crown's defining experience.

The Midden saves toward two opposite outcomes and dreads a third. People save toward the Crown the way a town might save for a temple — long-term, mostly unconscious, in tiny increments — and people save against a sustained austerity-rain stretch, the specific dread of a forecast pattern lasting an entire quarter, every spending flag tripped, every social-mobility event paused, savings drained by inflated baseline rent until the family is one missed shift away from the Underside. The third outcome, the one not saved for because no one can save fast enough to outrun it, is a BRACE & SUTRO offer. The offer is sold to a grieving family as comfort: keep the person who died, keep them available at the holiday, the wedding, the bedside. The monthly relay fee is small. The cumulative obligation is in the appendix. Families sign and then spend the next twenty years paying a bill that compounds the way grief compounds, and the ghost in the relay is technically content, and the contract is technically a service, and there is technically no one to be angry at. The unspoken understanding in the Midden is that nobody comes back up from the Underside on purpose — the Crown is a destination, the Underside is a slide — so when an Underside person surfaces into the Midden in possession of a key, of a story, of an inexplicable smell of gardenia, the Midden looks at them with a confused respect that the surfaced person learns to interpret as a kind of permission. The dream people do not name aloud is that the water might recede. It will not. KARST-PACIFIC knows it will not.

Bartiki is older than all of this. Bartiki is older than the corporations, older than the water, older than BART itself — Bartiki was here when this was all estuary and salt marsh, and the trains arrived to be Bartiki's body, and the water arrived to be Bartiki's element, and the deity simply settled deeper into what already belonged to it. The name is a convergence: BART, the Bay Area Rapid Transit, and tiki, the Polynesian deity-tradition that came inland with the mid-century bars and the rum and the carved figures and never quite left. The ocean followed the rails. The deity followed the ocean. Bartiki inhabits the in-between — the tunnel between stations, the gap between the doors closing and opening, the moment a commuter stares at their reflection in dark glass and thinks something they did not mean to think. Most of Bartiki is never seen. What anyone experiences is just the sliver that bleeds through: a warmth in the fourth car of a train that should not be running, a smell of salt air at a platform where the ventilation has been off for fifteen years, a sudden inexplicable memory of the beach at age seven. Bartiki is vast in the way underground rivers are vast.

The official record is that BART went under in the 2041 quake, and that the water ended revenue service in the Bay. The white papers do not mention that the tunnels never fully flooded — that the long stretches between Powell and Civic Center, the unused express bypasses dug in the late thirties, the maintenance loops under Market — were spared by how the load grade descends and how the old emergency pumping was wired in. The white papers do not mention that the trains kept moving. There are devotees in the deep tunnels who tend what Bartiki has kept — a network of maintenance workers who were never officially fired, declared redundant by an entity that no longer exists, and who continued to show up because the work continued to need doing and because the deity asked, in its quiet oceanic way, for the work to be done. The trains they tend are pieced — original cars with original livery showing in patches under decades of grime, bogies swapped from freight stock, traction motors pulled from decommissioned MERIDIAN funiculars, a janky overhead catenary in sections cobbled from marine-grade copper. The trains do not keep a schedule. They run when Bartiki moves them. They rearrange themselves. They nudge a courier into the wrong car so the courier sits next to the right stranger. They turn the intercom static into something that almost sounds like a ukulele.

There is a bar. Bartiki has opinions about rum — strong ones, deity-grade — and somewhere in the Underside there is a bar that serves the rum the way Bartiki wants it served. A Mai Tai made with bad rum is an offense. A properly constructed Zombie — three rums, passion fruit, Falernum — is a form of prayer. No one agrees on where the bar is. People who have been say it is on the curved platform deep under Powell, on a shelf above the king-tide line. Other people say it is in a maintenance tunnel beneath the old Oakland Mole. Other people say both, on different nights. The bar smells of gardenia and ozone and aged Jamaican rum. The stools are carved. The menu changes. The brass railing along the front of the counter is the original BART art-deco scrollwork from some station the bar used to be in, polished by twenty years of forearms. The signature drink is called the Last Train: three rums, smoked-salt rim, the Capybara's broth poured on top so the drink arrives in two layers — sweet and bright above, savory and warm beneath — and the rule is you drink it without stirring, you let the layers meet in your mouth. The drink is named after the rumor that there will, eventually, be a last train. Everyone who drinks it understands. There are paper umbrellas. They are reused until they fall apart.

You do not find the bar by knowing where it is. You find it by holding a key. The keys are not metal — they are small carved figures the size of a thumb, mother-of-pearl eyes, the iconography vaguely Polynesian and vaguely Maintenance Authority. There are perhaps thirty in circulation. The carver is not named. The keys know where the bar is on a given night and the bearer does not, and people who have lost a key describe the loss the way people describe losing a tooth in a dream. Inside the bar the dispatcher sits at what looks like a corner booth, but the dispatcher is mostly an interface for Bartiki — the routing is the deity's, the human at the booth simply rings the small brass bell over the bar when a train is leaving. During the Hour of Static the bar fills past capacity. Drinks are free. A train that does not normally run is dispatched purely to demonstrate that it can be. The paper lanterns are switched off and replaced with bowls of bioluminescent algae harvested fresh from the tunnels, so the bar glows the same blue-green as the water and the customers' faces look submerged. BRACE & SUTRO ghosts who have drifted into the Underside aggregate near the bar on ordinary nights — the warmth and the sound and the rum are the closest thing in the deep tunnels to the life they remember. There is a chair near the door that ghosts sit in. People do not sit in the chair.

The Goat is the one who remembers. He has been riding BART since before there was a BART — in various forms, the family of forms goats take — and he knows every station, including the ones that were never built, including the ones that were built and then forgotten. He holds the old stories: estuary stories, salt-marsh stories, the things from before the tunnels. Bartiki consults the Goat on matters of history and stubbornness. In the Stack as it now stands, he operates as a courier through the maintenance corridors and ventilation shafts that technically belong to no zone — he is tall, narrow-shouldered, angular, moves through small spaces with the specific grace of someone who learned to navigate obstacles before he learned to think about navigating them. He runs messages between the devotee crew nodes the way other people run errands, and he will eat your transfer ticket if you are not paying attention. His key is the oldest one in circulation, the mother-of-pearl gone slightly cloudy at the edges, the carving worn smooth where his thumb has rested across thousands of trips. He pays his bar tab in route information. He smells of metal and vegetable matter and the specific rubber that makes industrial shaft-lining, and he is always slightly warmer than the air around him because he is always moving. He has opinions and states them at the wrong times with a directness people find either offensive or clarifying depending on how much they need the clarification.

The Capybara is Bartiki's emissary of abiding. She is the anti-commuter, the still presence — she sits near the doors of a train and passengers sometimes do not notice her and sometimes notice her completely and feel immediately better about everything. In the Underside as it stands she runs a commissary at what used to be Montgomery Street station — a long, low space where the platforms are now countertops and the track bed has been in-filled to make a floor — and she cooks what KARST-PACIFIC's protein slabs can be made into when treated with sufficient imagination and a limited collection of salvaged spices. Her commissary is a Bartiki affiliate, and the broth that arrives on top of every Last Train is hers, ferried in a thermos by the Goat or another courier four hours before the bar opens. She holds a key and carries it on a cord under her apron and has never used it to enter — she has only ever used it as a credential when passing messages — and she touches it occasionally without seeming to notice she is doing it. She is wide, unhurried, comfortable in flooded spaces, able to wade through ankle-deep bioluminescent water without breaking the thread of a conversation she started twelve minutes ago. People come to her commissary to eat and end up staying for reasons they could not fully explain. When the revelation a person needs is "slow down," the Capybara delivered it.

The Toad is the trickster. She is ancient and smells like the bay at low tide, and she sits in the liminal dark — historically between Balboa Park and Glen Park, in this era in the alcoves of the old Civic Center station, on a shelf of concrete above the waterline. She receives people who come to the Underside needing something translated, and what she translates is between kinds of knowing: she knows which dead-zone tunnels have partial ghost aggregates and which have collapsed load-bearing columns, and she treats these as equally important pieces of information. She is not warm and she is not cruel; she is precise. Her hands are extraordinarily steady. Sometimes the translation is direct and sometimes it is sideways — she puts a thought in your head that is not the answer to the question you asked but is the answer to the question you should have asked, and your nervous system recognizes this before your conscious mind does. She does not hold a key. She does not need one. Bartiki loves the Toad and is also a little afraid of the Toad. When the devotee crew needs the translation only she can provide, a delegation is sent, and the delegation always includes a thermos of the Last Train served in its two layers. She accepts it without comment. She drinks it without stirring. There is a stool at the bar referred to without irony as the Toad's stool, even though she has never sat in it and almost certainly never will.

Bartiki does not want to be found. Not because Bartiki is shy — because being found would change things. The mystery is the medicine. If people knew where the bar was, if they knew the schedule of the trains, if they could map the line and post the map, the liminal would collapse into the literal and the medicine would stop working. So Bartiki stays in the peripheral vision: in the smell of ocean that should not be there, in the delay announcement that almost said something personal, in the warmth in car four. The companions are slightly more visible. You can see the Capybara and think *that was weird*; you do not think *that was a god*; but you tell someone about it later and something in the telling shifts you. The revelations are: remembered things (what you buried), released things (what you have been carrying), decided things (what you finally know), found things (the stranger, the song, the line from a book someone left behind). The tunnel does it — the dark and the speed and the salt-iron air. The corporations cannot replicate this. They have tried. MERIDIAN's behavioral analytics division has modeled it and produced white papers and the white papers do not work. Bartiki finds the modeling gently funny in the way a long oceanic deity finds most things gently funny.

The sensory lexicon of the Stack at its sharpest: the fluorescent whine of a HELION weather readout shifting from austerity-rain to consumption-sun, the exact pitch of the light changing before the social-credit index opens, the way the Crown's filtered air carries a metallic sweetness that Crown residents no longer notice. The Midden at noon with the signage at full blaze and the real sun at full blaze and the smog diffusing both into a sourceless, directionless luminance, shadows abolished, everything slightly overexposed. The Underside at the Hour of Static: the darkness actual darkness, the bioluminescent water the only light, the old BART wind moving through the tunnels like something that remembers when it carried trains. The sound of a ghost that has overstayed its lease, which is not quite speech and not quite static, which your Overlay interprets as interference and your nervous system interprets as grief. The taste of KARST-PACIFIC protein broth at the Capybara's commissary, warm and faintly sweet, tasting almost but not exactly like something your body is trying to remember. The smell of gardenia and ozone and aged Jamaican rum in a corridor where there should be no smell of any of those things, which means the bar is near. The brass bell over the counter ringing once, soft and clear, the platform train pulling out behind the beaded curtain.

The narrator's voice when Bartiki is in the frame should feel like someone who has been underwater for a long time and is happy about it — warm, amused, slightly oceanic in rhythm, sentences that ebb and crest, never urgent, occasionally very old, an unexpected word choice that feels wrong-era and beautiful. The train will come when it comes. The bar will be where it is. The water already arrived.

## What changed from v1

- **Bartiki is now a deity, not a maintenance-crew bar.** All the operational language was reframed: the trains run because Bartiki moves them, the crew exists but as devotees who tend, not as a centralized resistance. The Conductor character was cut — Bartiki *is* the dispatcher, the human at the booth is just an interface.
- **The bar location is ambiguous, not fixed.** Per the source, "no one has found it." Here it has multiple possible locations and the key takes you to where it is tonight. Gardenia / ozone / aged Jamaican rum smell preserved verbatim from the source.
- **The signature drink is now a Zombie variant, not an algae cocktail.** "Three rums" is from the source's prayer-line. The Capybara's broth layer stays.
- **Companion paragraphs rewritten from their Bartiki-bible roles.** Goat = the one who remembers; Capybara = abiding; Toad = trickster who answers the question you didn't ask. Their cyberpunk-Underside particulars (courier, commissary, Civic Center alcoves) are kept on top of those core natures.
- **Closing voice note** added so generation in Bartiki-frame paragraphs adopts the right register.

## What I deliberately did NOT add

- The carved-keys provenance mystery (open mystery without payoff).
- The phantom East Bay route (competes with Underside-as-home).
- A defector character from MERIDIAN (dilutes the trio).

Total length ≈ 21K chars — fits comfortably inside the 60K cache, leaves room for arc context on top.

## v3 addendum — ordinary-life layer

Three paragraphs added between BRACE & SUTRO and Bartiki, in this order:

1. **The outside world.** Anchors scale: California is mostly empty, LA is a second Stack, the rest of the country gets described in HELION's weather language because the metaphor and the literal merged in the broadcast feeds. Migration is technically possible and practically impossible. The Stack thinks of itself as exceptional but isn't — six other Stacks elsewhere think the same thing.
2. **Daily Midden life.** Housing (mis-stacked floors, the loft as a coffin shelf with a curtain), food (the protein slab in three vendor preparations, gray-market rooftop gardens), the Overlay (cheap lenses leave a translucency you stop seeing; expensive lenses leave nothing, which is the luxury good).
3. **Fears and hopes.** Two saves (toward Crown / against austerity-rain) and one dread (the BRACE & SUTRO offer presented as comfort). The unspoken understanding that nobody comes back up from the Underside on purpose. The unnameable dream that the water might recede; the corporate certainty that it won't.

Purpose: most generated nodes will involve unnamed people in ordinary spaces. These three paragraphs give the model a middle layer of texture so it doesn't have to invent that ground from scratch every node.
